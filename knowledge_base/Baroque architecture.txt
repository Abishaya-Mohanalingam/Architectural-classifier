Baroque Architecture (c. 1600-1750)
Grand, dramatic, highly ornamented design meant to inspire awe: curved
forms, sculptural decoration, strong light/shadow contrast, and a sense of
movement across the facade. In England closely associated with Christopher
Wren and Nicholas Hawksmoor, particularly the post-1666 rebuilding of London
churches including St Paul's Cathedral. Typical features: large domes, grand
staircases, richly painted ceilings. Conservation concerns include dome and
vault stability, cleaning/repairing carved stone ornament, and protecting
painted plasterwork from damp and light damage.
Baroque architecture was a highly decorative and theatrical building style that emerged in Italy during the early 17th century. It spread quickly across Europe and the Americas, lasting until the mid-18th century. Born out of the Catholic Church’s Counter-Reformation, Baroque design was intentionally used to overwhelm the senses, project power, and inspire religious awe in everyday people. While Renaissance architecture emphasized order, symmetry, and logic, the Baroque style championed drama, movement, and emotional intensity. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] 
## The Illusion of Movement and Space
The defining characteristic of Baroque architecture is the rejection of flat surfaces and straight lines. Architects treated walls as flexible sculptures, curving them inward and outward to create undulating facades. Buildings featured alternating concave and convex shapes that made structures appear as if they were moving. [6, 7, 8, 9, 10] 
To heighten this sense of drama, architects utilized the oval or ellipse as their primary floor plan. Ovals replaced the rigid circles and squares of the Renaissance, creating dynamic, unpredictable spaces that drew visitors forward. Domes became taller and more complex, often painted with breathtaking sky scenes using a technique called trompe l'œil (fool the eye). This style blended painting and architecture together, creating the optical illusion that the ceiling was opening up directly into heaven. [11, 12, 13, 14, 15] 
## Masters of Light and Shadow
Baroque buildings were designed like theatrical stages, relying heavily on chiaroscuro—the sharp contrast between light and dark. Architects achieved this by placing deep recesses, projecting columns, and heavy moldings on exterior walls. When sunlight hit these uneven surfaces, it cast intense, shifting shadows that changed the look of the building throughout the day. Inside, hidden windows were cut into domes to flood specific altars with dramatic beams of light, guiding the eyes of worshipers to focal points. [16, 17, 18, 19, 20] 
## Key Visual Characteristics

* Grand Staircases: Sweeping, monumental entryways designed for grand entrances and a sense of royal arrival.
* Solomonic Columns: Distinctive spiraled or twisted stone columns that added a dynamic energy to altars and entryways.
* Luxurious Materials: Heavy use of colorful marble, gilded gold accents, and bronze ornaments to showcase immense wealth.
* Broken Pediments: Decorative structures above doors and windows where the top arch or triangle is split open, often framing a statue. [21, 22, 23, 24, 25] 

## Famous Masterpieces
The epicenters of Baroque creation were Rome and France. In Italy, Francesco Borromini designed the church of San Carlo alle Quattro Fontane, a triumph of tightly packed, curving walls. Concurrently, Gian Lorenzo Bernini created the vast, welcoming arms of the colonnade enclosing St. Peter’s Square. In France, the style took on a grand, secular form at the Palace of Versailles, where the Hall of Mirrors used massive glass panes to multiply light and project the absolute power of King Louis XIV. [26, 27, 28, 29, 30] 
Baroque architecture eventually evolved into the even more ornate Rococo style. However, its lasting legacy remains its pioneering fusion of light, sculpture, and geometry into a unified, emotional experience. [31, 32, 33, 34, 35]
