Novelty Architecture (early 20th century-present)
Buildings shaped like the product or service they advertise (a giant donut,
a boot-shaped shop, a duck-shaped building) or built purely to attract
attention along a roadside. Rose alongside car culture in the US and spread
internationally as a marketing device. Often built from cheap, fast
materials (stucco over wood or wire frames), which creates real conservation
difficulty: many surviving examples were never intended to last decades and
require frequent structural intervention and material replacement to
survive at all.
Novelty architecture—also known as programmatic or mimetic architecture—is a playful building style where structures are given unusual, literal shapes for marketing and entertainment. Instead of relying on traditional geometric forms, these buildings are sculpted to look like everyday objects, animals, or food items. Emerging prominently alongside the rise of automobile culture in the 1920s and 1930s, this style turns the building itself into a giant, three-dimensional billboard designed to catch the eye of fast-moving drivers. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] 
## Form Follows Advertisement
While mainstream architecture focuses on spatial theory, structural purity, or social utility, novelty architecture focuses strictly on commercial attraction. It flips the modernist rule of "form follows function" into "form follows advertisement." If a business sells donuts, the building is shaped like a giant donut. If it sells shoes, the storefront is a massive boot. [6, 7] 
Historically, this style thrived along American highways like Route 66. As families began taking long road trips, roadside businesses needed a radical way to stand out against competing gas stations, diners, and motels. A building shaped like a giant coffee pot or a two-story bulldog naturally forced drivers to slow down, pull over, and take photos, serving as a highly effective marketing tool long before the digital age.
## Two Main Categories
Architectural historians generally divide novelty buildings into two distinct categories:

* Mimetic Architecture: Buildings that physically resemble the actual products or services sold inside. Examples include a fish market shaped like a giant whale or a real estate office shaped like a castle. [8] 
* Programmatic Architecture: Buildings shaped like objects completely unrelated to the business inside, used purely for shock value and spectacle. Examples include a corporate headquarters shaped like a picnic basket or a museum shaped like a giant toilet. [9, 10, 11] 

## Famous Historic Examples
One of the most famous early examples is The Big Duck in New York, built in 1931 by a duck farmer to sell eggs and poultry. It became so famous that architectural theorists coined the term "duck" to describe any building where the structure itself represents its function. [12, 13, 14, 15, 16] 
Another iconic landmark is the Longaberger Basket Company headquarters in Ohio, designed as a massive, 160-ton replica of the company’s best-selling maple wood basket, complete with heated handles. In the world of entertainment, the Lucy the Elephant structure in New Jersey stands six stories high and has served as a real estate office, a tavern, and a summer home since the late 19th century. [17, 18, 19] 
## Cultural Legacy
With the expansion of high-speed interstate highways and stricter local zoning laws in the late 20th century, novelty architecture faded from the mainstream. Critics often dismissed it as cheap, kitschy, or low-art. However, it paved the way for the extravagant, themed architecture of modern Las Vegas and global amusement parks. Today, these surviving roadside giants are celebrated as precious pieces of pop-culture history, proving that architecture can be deeply accessible, humorous, and undeniably fun. [20]
